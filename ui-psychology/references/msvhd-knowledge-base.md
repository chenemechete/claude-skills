## M — Message (Copywriting)

**Core principle:** Copy answers the question: *"Why should I care, right now?"*

### 3 Common Mistakes
- **Too long** — blocks of text cause users to skim or leave
- **Too generic** — vague copy leaves users uninterested
- **Unnecessary & duplicated** — extra sentences hide what matters

### 4 Practical Principles
1. **What's in it for me?** — Benefit must be obvious within 1–2 seconds
2. **Reassure** — Address doubts: "You can change this later," "No prep needed"
3. **Use specific & action words** — Clear verbs, concrete outcomes
4. **Talk like a real person** — Write how you'd speak to a friend; read it aloud

### Key Concepts
- **The Copy Swap Test:** Remove all logos and visuals. Could a competitor use the same words? If yes, the copy is too vague.
- **Harry Dry's Three Rules:** Is it visualizable? Is it falsifiable? Can nobody else say it?
- Write with your eraser — cut every unnecessary word
- No one cares what you can do; everyone cares what you can do *for them*
- Avoid "landing page words" — unlock, unleash, enhance, empower, supercharge
- Think "Call-to-value" not "Call-to-action" — "Create Your Website" beats "Sign Up Now"
- Kaplan's Law: any word not working *for* you is working *against* you
- A paragraph is ready when nothing can be removed — like a Jenga tower

### Internal Checklist
- Is the benefit obvious?
- Is the copy short and focused?
- Are the words specific and concrete?
- Do buttons use clear action verbs?
- Have doubts been anticipated and reassured?
- Is anything unnecessary or repeated?
- Does it sound like a real person?

## S — Structure (Layout)

**Core principle:** Structure is how you create clarity without shouting. Your brain groups information *before* it reads it — to save mental effort.

### 6 Structure Principles (Gestalt-based)
1. **Similarity** — Same role → same look. Standardize styles, sizes, components
2. **Proximity** — Tight spacing within groups, larger spacing between groups
3. **Simplicity** — Remove styling that doesn't communicate meaning; one primary action per region
4. **Alignment** — Pick a grid and commit. Left edges do most of the work. Use 4px spacing steps
5. **Common Region** — Group content with sections/cards. Prefer subtle backgrounds over heavy borders
6. **Continuity** — Design an obvious scan path (top-left → bottom). No zig-zag scanning

### 3 Common Mistakes
- **Sloppy spacing** — start with "too much" padding then adjust
- **Border bloat** — define areas with subtle container colors, not lines
- **Content cramming** — remove elements, use progressive disclosure

### Key Concepts
- **F-Pattern:** In the absence of strong visual cues, users default to scanning in an F-shape — heavy on the top and left, trailing off right and down. Good structure prevents this by giving the eye clear signals.

### Internal Checklist
- Are related elements clearly grouped?
- Can you see clear zones at a glance?
- Is everything aligned to a simple grid?
- Is spacing tighter inside groups than between groups?
- Does the reading order feel natural?
- Is low-value stuff kept out of prime real estate?
- Would grouping still work without borders or boxes?

## V — Visibility (Accessibility)

**Core principle:** Visibility isn't just compliance — it means your UI works when someone is tired, rushed, one-handed, or has a permanent or situational limitation.

### 3 Types of Disability to Design For
- **Permanent** — long-term limitations
- **Temporary** — short-term limitations (broken arm, eye infection)
- **Situational** — "life is happening" (one-handed, bright sunlight, distracted)

### 3 Core Visibility Principles
1. **Visible without searching** — Main action findable without scrolling, digging, or guessing
2. **Operable without precision** — Hittable even with reduced thumb accuracy, fatigue, or motion impairment
3. **Actionable without guessing** — Actions look like actions; no guessing required

### 7 Common Mistakes
- Tiny & close targets
- Low contrast text (minimum 4.5:1 for normal text; 3:1 for large text 24px+)
- Actions that don't look clickable
- Missing hints for hidden content
- Color-only meaning (1 in 12 men are color blind)
- Too many patterns in one view
- "They'll figure it out" assumptions

### Key Concepts
- **Error prevention:** Most visibility failures are slip-ups — people know what to do but the UI makes it easy to do the wrong thing. Disable invalid states, always offer undo, confirm destructive actions.

### Internal Checklist
- Key action visible without opening menus or scrolling?
- Are primary actions big enough and spaced apart?
- Sufficient contrast for text and icons?
- Is meaning conveyed beyond color alone?
- Do controls clearly look interactive?
- Is there a hint when content overflows?
- Are destructive actions separated and confirmed?
- Could a tired, distracted, one-handed person still succeed?

## H — Hierarchy (Emphasis)

**Core principle:** Hierarchy is how you control attention — not how you decorate. Layout makes a screen understandable; Hierarchy makes its purpose *unmissable*.

**Key truth:** Hierarchy is relative — one element can only stand out if others step back. Most hierarchy comes from what you *turn down*, not what you turn up.

### The Foggy Glasses Test
Blur or squint at the screen. The most important element should still be obvious. If everything looks equally loud — hierarchy isn't working.

### The 6 Hierarchy Dials
1. **Size** — Make the primary message/action noticeably larger; turn *down* surrounding elements
2. **Color** — Use accent color for key actions; muted color to demote secondary details
3. **Space** — Whitespace isolates what matters; "buy" space by shrinking secondary elements
4. **Placement** — Prime real estate (top/first scan) for the primary message; action near the decision moment
5. **Visualization** — Show don't tell; progress bar > paragraph; use the familiar pattern
6. **Motion** — Reinforce the screen's goal; avoid eye candy; keep it subtle

### 3 Common Mistakes
- **Wrong dial** — changing color when the real issue is placement or space
- **Weak dial** — subtle adjustments where nothing stands out; be bolder with fewer changes
- **Screaming dial** — cranking every dial to max; establish a calm baseline, then choose 1–2 dials to lead

### Key Concepts
- **Von Restorff Effect:** When one element is noticeably different from its neighbors, people spot it faster and remember it better.
- **Reactance:** When interfaces feel overly forceful, people push back. If everything is emphasized, nothing is.

### Internal Checklist
- What's the first thing your eye hits?
- Does that focal point match the screen's main goal?
- Do primary actions clearly stand out from secondary ones?
- Does the blur test highlight the right elements?
- Are only a few things "shouting"?
- Is whitespace helping key elements stand out?
- Is any decoration stealing attention from what matters?

## D — Delight (Reward)

**Core principle:** Delight is the emotional outcome of a screen — what the user *feels*. The best delight is contextual + proportional: right feeling, right moment, right intensity.

### The 30-Second Delight Test
Users are silently asking one of:
- *"What's going on — am I safe?"* → needs **Control**
- *"Am I improving — did I do well?"* → needs **Competence**
- *"Do others see this — am I recognized?"* → needs **Recognition**

If your UI can't answer at least one, it will feel emotionally flat — even if it's perfectly usable.

### The Delight Trifecta (Self-Determination Theory)
- 🛡️ **Control** — User feels certain, safe, in command. Dominant emotion: Anxiety → Relief
- 🪴 **Competence** — User feels capable and improving. Dominant emotion: Effort → Achievement
- 🤝 **Recognition** — User feels seen and acknowledged. Dominant emotion: Pride → Being noticed

A screen can deliver a blend — landing anywhere on the triangle. Match the dominant emotion first.

### 3 Common Mistakes
- **Wrong delight** — delivering a payoff the user doesn't care about (e.g., "Congrats!" when they're anxious)
- **Shy delight** — reward exists but isn't visible or is too generic; surface it explicitly: "Saved 1 hour," "Delivered today"
- **Over-delight** — giant fullscreen confetti for every click; save big celebrations for real milestones

### Key Concepts
- **Emotional design layers (Don Norman):**
  - Visceral — first impression, automatic feeling
  - Behavioral — pleasure and effectiveness of use
  - Reflective — what it means to the user, story they tell about it

### Internal Checklist
- Did the user just do something meaningful?
- What should they feel: safe, progressing, or recognized?
- Is it 100% clear the action worked?
- What's the smallest touch that could create that feeling?
- Is the reward proportional to the moment?
- Will this still feel good the 10th time?
- Does this nudge a healthy, user-aligned habit?
